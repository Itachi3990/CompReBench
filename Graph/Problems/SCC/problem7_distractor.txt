The Federal Anti-Monopoly Commission is investigating a sprawling syndicate of international conglomerates suspected of illegal price-fixing. The structural complexity of this corporate empire is staggering, involving thousands of shell companies, holding groups, and subsidiary brands. During the preliminary phases of the investigation, junior forensic accountants were distracted by the sheer financial volume of the enterprise. They attempted to calculate the "maximum flow of dividends," obsessively tracking the precise stock prices in dollars and the quarterly dividend payouts moving from the subsidiaries up to the parent corporations. They assumed that by analyzing the maximum possible throughput of capital, they could identify the most critical holding companies in the network. 

Simultaneously, a team of human resources analysts from the Department of Labor proposed a "bipartite matching of executives to boards." They spent months trying to perfectly pair up thousands of individual C-suite executives with specific, available board seats across the various companies. They factored in the exact number of employees at each firm and the executives' past salary histories, believing that an optimized, one-to-one assignment of leadership was the key to untangling the corporate web. 

However, the lead anti-trust litigator immediately scrapped both of these financial and personnel-based models. While it might seem like the primary objective of a corporate investigation is to track the absolute maximum flow of money through the holding companies, or to perfectly assign executives to board seats to understand the hierarchy, these metrics completely miss the mechanics of modern corporate control. The true power in this syndicate does not flow like water, nor is it a simple matter of human resources. 

The control is exerted through a massive, directed graph of voting rights. Company A owns a 51% controlling stake in Company B, giving A the power to dictate B's actions. But B might own a controlling stake in C, and C might secretly own a controlling stake back in A through a blind trust. This creates a directed web of absolute influence. 

The litigator is NOT trying to calculate the maximum flow of dividends. They are NOT trying to perfectly pair executives with companies, and they are NOT trying to find the shortest path of influence to a specific CEO. 

Instead, the commission needs a structural analysis of the voting rights graph. They require an algorithm to process thousands of these directed ownership links and segment the entire corporate landscape into distinct, isolated factions. The algorithm must identify maximal groupings of companies where a terrifying condition holds true: within any identified group, every single company can eventually exert controlling influence over every other company in that exact same group through a chain of subsidiary ownership. By finding these maximal clusters of mutual, directed influence, the litigator can legally prove the existence of self-contained, monopolistic cartels operating under the radar, regardless of their stock prices or how much money flows between them.
