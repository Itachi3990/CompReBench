By the dawn of the 23rd century, humanity has established a sprawling, robust industrial presence across the entire Sol system. The United Nations Solar Administration (UNSA) oversees a vast network of planetary colonies, orbital habitats, asteroid mining outposts, and deep-space research stations. From the domed cities of Mars and the cloud-cities of Venus to the icy subsurface oceans of Europa and the methane lakes of Titan, the solar economy relies entirely on the constant, uninterrupted flow of massive cargo freighters. 

However, navigating the solar system is not like traversing an ocean on Earth. Space is not a flat plane, and the cost of travel is not simply measured in linear distance. The primary expense in interplanetary logistics is Delta-v—the change in velocity required to perform orbital maneuvers. Moving a massive freighter from a low Earth orbit to a geostationary transfer orbit, then initiating an injection burn to Mars, and finally circularizing the orbit upon arrival requires an astronomical amount of highly refined reaction mass. 

Every single planetary body, moon, and space station is connected by a web of complex orbital transfer windows. A direct burn from Earth to Jupiter might be possible, but it is often prohibitively expensive in terms of fuel. Instead, freighters frequently utilize multi-leg journeys, taking advantage of gravitational slingshots around intermediate bodies like Mars or Venus to conserve reaction mass, even if it significantly increases the travel time. A journey from Mercury to Pluto might involve five or six distinct orbital maneuvers and planetary flybys to minimize the total fuel expenditure.

The 'Sol-Logistics Consortium', a massive conglomerate of the system's largest shipping corporations, has realized that their current method of calculating flight paths is terribly inefficient. Currently, when a contract is posted to move raw materials from an asteroid mining rig to a Martian refinery, supercomputers calculate the optimal route for that specific request on demand. With tens of thousands of cargo requests being processed daily, this on-the-fly calculation creates massive computational bottlenecks and delays in the fast-paced commodities market.

To resolve this, the Consortium has commissioned a monumental data science project. They want to construct the 'Absolute Delta-v Matrix.' This is not a simple map, but a comprehensive, multi-dimensional database. The engineers must map out every single significant human settlement, space station, and automated mining rig in the solar system—thousands of distinct nodes. They must then analyze the orbital mechanics between every pair of these nodes. 

The ultimate goal of the project is to pre-calculate and store the absolute minimum Delta-v requirement (the lowest possible fuel cost) between every single possible pair of locations in the entire solar system. If an executive on Earth wants to know the cheapest way to send a shipment from a specific crater on the Moon to a specific floating platform on Saturn, they shouldn't have to wait for a supercomputer to run a complex orbital simulation. They should be able to query the Matrix and instantly receive the minimum fuel cost, allowing them to price their shipping contracts perfectly. 

This requires analyzing billions of potential multi-leg trajectories. The resulting database will be the backbone of the entire solar economy, allowing any shipping company, automated freighter, or trade official to instantly know the theoretical minimum cost to move goods between any two points in human space, revolutionizing commerce and logistics for billions of citizens.
