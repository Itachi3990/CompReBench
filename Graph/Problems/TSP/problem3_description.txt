Maintenance Tour

A maintenance crew has to inspect a set of facilities connected by weighted roads, service lanes, and access corridors. The locations are spread across a large operating area that mixes direct connections with indirect alternatives. Some routes are short and easy to understand, but they may not lead to the best full itinerary once every facility has to be included. Other routes look less efficient at first glance but can reduce the overall total when the entire network is considered together.

The maintenance team is not trying to make a partial pass through the easiest facilities. Every facility must be visited exactly one time, and the route has to end where it began. That means the plan must form one closed circuit that covers the whole network without repetition. If one facility is skipped, the inspection is incomplete. If one facility appears twice, the route no longer satisfies the rules. If the vehicle finishes anywhere other than the starting point, the circuit is not properly closed.

That structure is important because the crew often works under a fixed service window. Once the route begins, the order of visits determines whether the day’s maintenance can be completed before the shift ends. A circuit that looks slightly less direct can still be the right choice if it prevents an expensive return trip later. The team therefore needs a route that is globally efficient across the entire weighted network rather than one that simply feels short step by step. The final answer should identify the minimum-cost closed tour that touches every facility once and only once.

There is also a traceability requirement. After the route is chosen, the supervisor has to compare it against the maintenance list and confirm that every facility was included in the correct order. That means the solution cannot rely on vague reasoning about “roughly covering” the area. It needs to be a precise cycle that can be audited afterward. The route must begin at one facility, pass through all the others once each, and return to the start with the smallest total cost available in the graph.

The practical setting matters because the crew is usually working under time pressure. A facility near the entrance may be easy to reach, but choosing it too early could force an expensive return later. A route that seems tidy on the first few steps can become awkward once the remaining facilities are taken into account. That is why the decision has to be based on the full weighted graph rather than a local guess. The best answer is the inspection circuit with the smallest total cost among all valid circuits.

The supervisors want something they can record and audit later. They should be able to follow the ordered list of facilities and see that each one appears exactly once, with the path finally looping back to the start. The goal is not to maximize coverage in some vague sense, because full coverage is already required. The real challenge is to choose the lowest-cost route that satisfies the coverage requirement while respecting the single-visit rule and the return-to-origin rule.

In short, the desired output is one complete tour through all facilities, arranged as a simple cycle, with the least possible total travel weight. The route should be globally optimal across the weighted network, not just locally convenient.
