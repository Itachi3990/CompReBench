Field Survey Circuit

A research team is moving between observation sites scattered across a remote area of hills, service tracks, and rough access roads. The sites are not arranged in any neat line. Some are close together on a ridge, some sit deeper in a valley, and some are separated by long connectors that only look sensible when the whole trip is considered. Each connection in the network has a weight attached to it, representing the effort, fuel, or time needed to move between two sites. That means the team cannot choose routes based only on physical distance or visual convenience. A path that looks short on the map might still be costly, while a more circuitous path may turn out to be the better choice overall.

The assignment is to build one complete circuit that passes through every site exactly once and returns to the point where the survey began. The crew is not trying to inspect just the most important places, and they are not trying to find a route that merely reaches the farthest site. The requirement is stricter than that. The route has to cover the entire set of locations without repetition, because duplicate visits would waste time and break the structure of the survey plan. The final stop must bring the team back to the start so the trip can be closed out cleanly.

The reason the team insists on the full circuit is that the survey results are only meaningful when the entire region is covered in one coherent sequence. The crew’s vehicles carry measuring gear that must be moved carefully from site to site, and every unnecessary detour reduces the usefulness of the day’s work. A route that reuses a site would not just waste effort; it would also make the field notes harder to interpret later. The team therefore wants a route that is both complete and simple in structure: one visit per site, one return to the starting point, and one globally cheapest circuit over the weighted network.

In practice, the survey team works with limited daylight, a fixed fuel budget, and a map that includes a mixture of obvious and deceptive choices. A road that leads through a dry basin may look efficient but connect badly to the next site. Another road might cross a longer plateau yet open the door to several cheaper downstream transitions. The proper answer therefore depends on the total cost of the full circuit, not on the attractiveness of any one segment. The crew needs the least expensive complete tour across the graph, even if some of its individual steps are not the most obvious ones.

The logistics notes also emphasize that the route should be straightforward to explain afterward. A field supervisor should be able to trace the order of visits from the initial site to the final return and verify that each site appears once and only once. If any site is omitted, the survey is incomplete. If a site is revisited, the route is no longer a simple circuit. If the journey ends somewhere other than the start, the team has failed to close the loop. The only valid outcomes are complete closed routes that satisfy all three conditions at once.

This problem is therefore about a global decision over the weighted network. The team is not asked to compute local best steps in isolation. They need the best overall circuit through all sites, and the best circuit is the one with the minimum total travel cost among all routes that visit each site exactly once and return to the origin.
