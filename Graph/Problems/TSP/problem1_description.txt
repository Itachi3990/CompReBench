Night Delivery Loop

The city is at its most complicated after dark. Traffic lights still blink at empty intersections, warehouse shutters continue to rattle in the wind, and loading docks along the river keep a steady mechanical rhythm long after the retail streets have gone quiet. In that setting, a courier company has to plan one complete overnight trip across several depots connected by weighted roads. The depots are spread across different neighborhoods, each with its own travel quirks. One road may be short but slow because it passes through a narrow market street. Another may be longer but surprisingly cheap because it follows a service corridor that avoids congestion. A third might cross a bridge with a high toll, making it unattractive unless the full route is considered carefully.

The dispatch team is not asking for a random walk through the city. They want one route that can be written down cleanly, checked by a supervisor, and executed without improvisation. Every depot in the network must be visited exactly once, and the vehicle must return to the depot where it started. That means the route has to form one closed loop that includes the whole set without repeating any location along the way. A path that looks convenient at first can turn out to be a bad choice later if it forces a costly detour near the end, so the planner has to reason about the entire circuit rather than one segment at a time.

There is also a practical reason the team prefers a single orderly circuit. The dispatch office tracks inventory transfers, driver time, and gate access in one continuous log, so the route must be easy to verify from beginning to end. A supervisor should be able to compare the final itinerary against the depot list and confirm that no location was skipped and no location was entered twice. The exact order matters because the cost of one move affects the options that remain for the next move. A route that seems acceptable in the first few hops may become impossible or expensive later if it strands the vehicle far from the remaining depots.

Several practical notes make the assignment feel realistic. The vehicle leaves fully loaded, so every unnecessary kilometer matters. The team wants to avoid repeated passes through the same part of town, not because the map forbids it in ordinary driving, but because the route itself must remain simple and unambiguous. The route should be legible enough that a night supervisor could follow it from start to finish and see exactly how the vehicle covers the network. If one depot is skipped, the trip is incomplete. If the vehicle returns to the start too early, the circuit is broken. If the same depot is entered twice, the route no longer satisfies the rules.

The environment adds a little extra tension. A mild fog sometimes settles near the river. A road crew may close one lane without notice. A delivery gate can remain open slightly longer than expected. Those details change the convenience of individual streets, but they do not change the main question. The goal is still to find the cheapest valid closed route that visits every depot exactly one time and ends where it began. The answer should therefore reflect a complete circuit through the weighted network, with the total travel cost as small as possible.

This is not a problem about mapping the city block by block, and it is not about building a tree of preferred roads. It is about committing to one ordered sequence of visits that satisfies all the constraints simultaneously. The only acceptable route is a full tour of the depots, one visit per depot, with a return to the origin at the end and the minimum possible total cost among all such tours.
