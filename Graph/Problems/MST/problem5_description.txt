Deep beneath the frozen tundra of Svalbard, a global consortium of technology corporations and sovereign governments is constructing 'Project Oracle,' a planetary-scale quantum artificial intelligence intended to solve humanity's most complex challenges, from climate modeling to curing diseases. Unlike traditional supercomputers, Oracle is not housed in a single server room. Instead, its immense processing power is distributed across thousands of massive, distinct computational silos buried deep within the bedrock, spread over a subterranean area the size of a small city. 

For Oracle to achieve true artificial general intelligence, it cannot operate as a collection of isolated processors. The thousands of silos must be inextricably linked, functioning together as a single, cohesive, unified synthetic brain. To achieve this, the consortium plans to connect the silos using a state-of-the-art network of cryo-cooled superconducting quantum fiber-optic cables. These cables are capable of transmitting quantum states instantaneously, but they are incredibly fragile and astonishingly expensive to manufacture and lay underground.

Geological survey teams have mapped the subterranean environment and identified a vast web of potential pathways for laying these quantum cables between various pairs of silos. However, the cost of excavating and installing a cable along any given pathway is highly variable. The expense is determined by a multitude of factors: the physical distance between the silos, the density and hardness of the intervening rock formations, the presence of underground aquifers or tectonic fault lines that must be bypassed or reinforced against, and the immense energy overhead required to maintain the absolute zero temperatures needed for the superconducting shielding.

The consortium has compiled a detailed database assigning a precise 'installation and thermal overhead cost' to every single possible cable route. The project managers are now faced with a monumental optimization problem. They must guarantee that every single computational silo is integrated into the primary network. Data must be able to flow freely from any silo to any other silo in the entire complex, allowing the AI's neural pathways to distribute tasks and share memory globally across the facility. An isolated silo would represent a massive waste of processing power and could potentially lobotomize a key function of the Oracle AI.

However, the project is teetering on the edge of its colossal budget, and the thermal management systems for the cryo-cables are already near their maximum capacity. To prevent budget overruns and catastrophic thermal runaway, the project directors have instituted a draconian efficiency mandate. The total combined cost of all the selected cable routes must be the absolute mathematical minimum required to achieve full connectivity. 

To achieve this, the engineers have been instructed that absolutely no redundant connections are to be built. If a path for data already exists between Silo Alpha and Silo Beta through a series of intermediate silos, laying a direct cable between them is strictly prohibited. The final network architecture must not contain any closed loops or circular data pathways. The design must be a minimally connected structure—a delicate, tree-like web that reaches every single node in the facility without a single wasted strand of quantum fiber. The success of Project Oracle, and potentially the future of technological advancement, relies entirely on finding this single, perfectly optimized, lowest-cost configuration.
