In the mountainous, isolated region of the Serene Valley, a recently formed coalition of municipal governments is attempting a massive infrastructure overhaul. For decades, the dozens of small towns and remote logging camps scattered across the valley have relied on unreliable, localized diesel generators. The coalition has finally secured funding to build a unified, valley-wide electrical grid, drawing power from a massive new hydroelectric dam at the northern edge. When the project was first announced, independent consultants flooded the planning committee with misguided proposals. Several consulting firms submitted elaborate plans focused on the "shortest path for the electricity," obsessively measuring the physical distance from the main hydroelectric dam to each individual house in the valley. They treated the grid like a series of isolated drag races, trying to ensure that power reached each specific home with the absolute lowest latency.

Other engineering firms were fixated on the "maximum voltage flow." They spent months calculating the precise voltage capacities of different wire gauges and the resistance generated by the mountainous terrain, assuming that the ultimate goal was to construct a massive, over-engineered conduit capable of pushing the maximum possible electrical load into the valley all at once. They treated the infrastructure project like an industrial pipeline intended to flush an entire reservoir of electricity into the towns.

However, both of these approaches ignored the severe economic realities of the Serene Valley. The coalition’s budget is incredibly tight, and industrial-grade high-tension copper wire is staggeringly expensive. While it might seem like the primary objective is to find the fastest, shortest route from the dam to the furthest logging camp, or to maximize the sheer volume of power that can be transmitted across the network, these metrics are luxuries the coalition cannot afford. 

The true challenge is purely financial and topological. The coalition has a survey map detailing every possible geographic route where power lines could theoretically be strung between the various towns and the dam, along with the exact, exorbitant cost of constructing the lines along each specific route due to the harsh terrain. 

The mandate from the coalition is strict: every single town and logging camp must be connected to the grid. No settlement can be left in the dark. However, the total, cumulative cost of purchasing and stringing the copper wire must be the absolute minimum possible amount. 

To achieve this, the engineers must adhere to a strict policy of zero redundancy. If there is already a sequence of power lines that connects Town A to Town B, building an additional, direct line between them is considered an unforgivable waste of copper. The final electrical network must be perfectly continuous, ensuring power can reach anywhere, but it must not contain any closed loops or circular circuits. 

Therefore, the coalition is NOT looking to calculate the shortest path from the dam to any specific location. They are NOT trying to maximize the overall voltage capacity of the system, and they are NOT trying to build a robust, highly redundant network that can survive multiple failures. Instead, they need an algorithm to analyze all potential construction routes and select the exact subset of connections that binds every single town together into one unified grid, while guaranteeing that the total, overall expenditure on copper wire is mathematically minimized.
