In the neon-drenched underworld of New Angeles, the "Shadow Broker" operates a heavily encrypted gig-economy network that matches elite cybernetic mercenaries, known as "Runners," with high-risk corporate extraction contracts. The sheer volume of illicit activity is staggering, and managing the assignments requires advanced computational oversight. In the early days of the network, the Broker's junior handlers focused almost exclusively on the financials and the chronology of the operations. They spent vast amounts of processing power trying to calculate a perfect "topological ordering of the contracts," attempting to sequence the jobs chronologically so that the fallout from one extraction wouldn't interfere with the next. 

Simultaneously, other handlers were obsessed with security audits, running complex algorithms dedicated to "detecting cycles of betrayal." They carefully mapped the shifting allegiances between the Runners and the various mega-corporations, terrified of assigning a Runner to a contract that might loop back and expose the Broker's own operations. They factored in the exact street distance in urban sectors between safehouses and calculated the maximum possible payout amounts in crypto-credits for every single job, assuming that maximizing the total monetary value of a single, long sequence of contracts was the ultimate measure of success.

However, the reality of the Shadow Broker’s business model is entirely different. While it might seem like the primary goal is to find the highest total payout across a chronological sequence of jobs, or to map the safest, cycle-free route through the city's treacherous alliances, these metrics ignore the fundamental bottleneck of the mercenary market. 

The core issue is that Runners are highly specialized, and so are the corporate contracts. One contract might require a decker with a military-grade icebreaker implant, while another might strictly require a heavy-weapons specialist with cybernetic recoil dampeners. A contract cannot be given to just anyone; it can only be accepted by a Runner who possesses the exact required skill tags. Furthermore, the rules of the shadow economy are absolute: a Runner can only execute one contract at a time, and a contract can only be assigned to a single Runner. The Broker has two entirely distinct pools of assets: the available, idle Runners waiting for work on one side, and the open, unassigned corporate contracts on the other.

The Broker is NOT trying to chronologically order the jobs. They are NOT trying to detect loops in the allegiance network, and they are NOT trying to maximize the crypto-credit payout of a single route. 

Instead, the Broker needs their matchmaking algorithm to look at the massive web of potential compatibilities between the two distinct groups. Some versatile Runners might be technically capable of handling five different open contracts, while highly specialized Runners might only be suited for one specific job. The algorithm must navigate these overlapping capabilities to determine the absolute maximum number of valid, exclusive pairings that can be executed simultaneously. The ultimate goal of the Shadow Broker is volume: they must identify the largest possible set of disjoint, one-to-one assignments between the mercenary pool and the contract pool, ensuring that the highest possible number of jobs are filled and executed concurrently without ever double-booking a single asset.
