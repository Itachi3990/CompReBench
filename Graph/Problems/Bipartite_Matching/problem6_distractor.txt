In the sprawling metropolis of Neo-Veridia, the central medical authority manages a highly complex, time-sensitive registry of potential organ donors and critically ill patients awaiting transplants. The logistical challenge is immense, spanning dozens of hospitals and clinics across the city. When the system was first implemented, the medical engineers were heavily focused on logistics. They spent millions developing algorithms to calculate the "shortest biological path" for transport, obsessively measuring the physical distance between hospitals in miles and optimizing the exact routes for the emergency courier drones to minimize transit time. Other teams were fixated on the "maximum blood flow capacity" of the organs themselves, calculating precise blood volumes in liters and pressure tolerances to ensure surgical viability, treating the entire process like a fluid dynamics optimization problem. 

They assumed that if they could just move the organs fast enough and guarantee their physical integrity, the crisis would be solved. They poured resources into finding the single fastest route from the central cryo-facility to the outer sector clinics, or trying to calculate the absolute maximum volume of biological material that could be transported in a single night. 

However, this logistical obsession entirely missed the core medical reality of transplant science. While it might seem like finding the absolute shortest distance to transport a heart, or maximizing the sheer volume of organs moved per hour is the primary objective, these metrics are completely useless if the underlying biology doesn't align. An organ cannot simply go to the closest patient; it is strictly gated by a complex matrix of tissue types, blood group compatibility, and genetic markers. 

The true problem is a massive, two-sided puzzle. On one side of the digital ledger is a group of available donors, each with a highly specific biological profile. On the other side is a distinct, separate group of waiting patients, each requiring an organ that perfectly matches their own unique biological parameters. A donor’s organ can only be given to a patient if their biological profiles are strictly compatible. Furthermore, the math is unforgiving: one donor provides exactly one heart, and one patient receives exactly one heart. A donor cannot be split among multiple patients, and a patient cannot receive organs from multiple donors simultaneously.

Therefore, the central medical AI is NOT trying to find the shortest route for the courier drones. It is NOT calculating the total fluid capacity of the biological material, and it is NOT trying to map a continuous circuit between all the hospitals. 

Instead, the AI must analyze the vast, complex web of biological compatibilities between the two distinct groups. Some donors have common blood types and might be compatible with many patients, while others have rare markers and match only one specific individual. The objective is to evaluate all these potential, valid links and construct a definitive assignment plan. The AI must determine the absolute maximum number of valid, one-to-one pairings that can be finalized between the donor pool and the patient pool. The only metric of success is maximizing the total count of these exclusive, disjoint surgical matches, ensuring that the highest possible number of lives are saved without violating the strict rules of biological compatibility.
