The global manufacturing conglomerate, Titan Dynamics, oversees an incredibly intricate supply chain for their flagship product, the atmospheric terraforming engine. The engine requires over eighty thousand distinct sub-components, manufactured across five continents by thousands of different subsidiary factories. When the production line first experienced mysterious, cascading delays, the logistics executives blamed transportation bottlenecks. They spent months agonizing over the "maximum flow of materials," calculating the precise tonnage of steel and rare earth metals that could be shipped per day, and measuring the exact physical distance in kilometers between the forging plants and the assembly hubs. They poured millions into expanding port capacities, assuming that if they could just maximize the physical throughput of the shipping routes, the delays would vanish.

Other analysts believed the problem lay in organizational silos. They deployed algorithms to map out "strongly connected components" within the corporate structure, trying to identify deeply insular clusters of factories that only traded parts with one another and refused to integrate with the broader supply chain. They tracked the financial value of every contract in millions of dollars, attempting to find the most valuable, isolated echo chambers of manufacturing to forcefully break them up. 

However, both of these massive, costly initiatives failed to resolve the core issue, because the true problem was hidden within the engineering blueprints themselves, not the logistics or the corporate politics. While it might seem like the paramount goal is to establish the maximum possible flow of raw materials through the network, or to isolate and break apart clusters of insular factories, the actual root cause of the crippling delays is a structural paradox in the design dependencies.

The production of the terraforming engine is governed by a strict hierarchy of prerequisites. For example, the magnetic containment housing cannot be fabricated until the superconducting coils have been delivered, and the coils cannot be wound until the synthetic sapphire cores are cast. These dependencies are strictly directional. 

The nightmare scenario that the chief engineer fears is that over decades of iterative, undocumented design revisions by different departments, a fatal flaw has been introduced into this web of dependencies. The true challenge is a binary structural audit. The engineering team suspects that somewhere deep in the blueprint registry, a component 'A' now requires a sub-component 'B' to be finished first, but sub-component 'B' requires 'C', and due to a catastrophic clerical error, 'C' has been marked as requiring 'A'. If this occurs, the automated factory systems will simply halt, waiting on each other forever in an inescapable deadlock. 

Therefore, the audit team is NOT trying to calculate the maximum flow of shipping containers. They are NOT trying to identify strongly connected corporate subsidiaries, and they are NOT measuring the physical distance between plants. 

Instead, they need an algorithm to ingest the entire, massive directed graph of engineering prerequisites. The algorithm's sole purpose is to perform a rigorous, binary safety check on the dependency structure. It must systematically follow the directional arrows of the requirements and determine one specific thing: does this massive network contain a closed repetition? The team simply needs a definitive yes-or-no answer regarding the existence of any closed loop anywhere in the dependency chain, no matter how deeply buried or complex, because even a single circular requirement will permanently paralyze the entire global production line.
