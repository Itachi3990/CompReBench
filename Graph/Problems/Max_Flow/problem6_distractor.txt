The coastal megacity of New Alexandria is facing an unprecedented crisis: a Category 6 hurricane is projected to make landfall in exactly forty-eight hours. The city's emergency management directorate has initiated a total evacuation protocol, aiming to move millions of residents from the vulnerable downtown sectors to the elevated, reinforced safe zones in the northern mountains. During the initial panic, several junior traffic analysts desperately tried to calculate the "shortest evacuation route" for the citizens. They obsessed over the physical distance in miles between the city center and the mountains, trying to map out the single most direct highway for everyone to use. They measured the exact travel time in seconds for a single vehicle traveling at maximum speed along various corridors.

Simultaneously, a team of urban planners suggested building a "minimum spanning tree of the highways," arguing that the city should ensure every single neighborhood was connected to the evacuation network using the absolute minimum total miles of open road, believing that keeping the active road network as sparse as possible would reduce confusion and accidents.

However, the seasoned director of emergency management immediately discarded both of these approaches. While it might seem like finding the absolute shortest, fastest path out of the city is the most logical priority during a crisis, or that maintaining a sparse, minimally connected road network is efficient, these strategies completely ignore the terrifying reality of a mass evacuation. If three million people try to use the single shortest highway simultaneously, the resulting gridlock will trap them all in the flood zone. 

The true problem is a matter of sheer volume versus hard constraints. The city's highway network is a complex, sprawling web of interstates, local roads, bridges, and tunnels. The critical factor is not how long a road is, but how wide it is. Every single segment of the road network has a strict, unyielding capacity limit based on the number of lanes—it can only handle a specific maximum number of vehicles per hour before traffic completely stalls. 

Therefore, the emergency directorate is NOT trying to find the shortest path to the safe zone. They are NOT trying to connect every neighborhood with the minimum total length of active road, and they are NOT trying to organize the evacuation chronologically. 

Instead, the director needs a comprehensive algorithm to analyze the entire, interwoven web of highways, bridges, and their specific lane capacities. The objective is to utilize every available route, funneling cars through detours, backroads, and multiple parallel interstates simultaneously. The algorithm must determine the absolute maximum number of vehicles that can be continuously pushed through the entire road network from the city center to the mountain safe zones every hour, without ever exceeding the lane capacity of any individual street or bridge. The only metric that matters is maximizing the total, aggregate volume of traffic safely escaping the city before the storm surge hits.
