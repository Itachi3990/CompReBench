A catastrophic financial contagion has struck the global markets. A massive regional banking sector, heavily over-leveraged in toxic derivatives, is on the verge of complete collapse. If this sector fails, it will trigger a domino effect, destroying the savings of millions and plunging the global economy into a devastating depression. The World Central Bank (WCB) has decided on a drastic intervention: an emergency injection of pure capital—liquidity—directly into the failing sector to stabilize their balance sheets. 

However, in the highly regulated and complex world of international finance, the WCB cannot simply wire a trillion dollars directly to the failing banks. The capital must flow through a strictly regulated, convoluted network of intermediary financial institutions. The WCB must distribute the funds to massive international clearing houses, which then pass the money to investment firms, which then transfer it to national reserve banks, which finally disburse it to the failing regional sector. 

The critical challenge is regulatory bottlenecks. Every single financial institution in this network operates under strict legal and operational limits regarding how much capital it can process, verify, and transfer in a single 24-hour period. These 'transfer capacities' are rigid. For example, Clearing House Alpha might be legally allowed to process 50 billion dollars a day, but it only has established financial channels to transfer 20 billion to Bank Beta and 10 billion to Investment Firm Gamma. 

The Chairman of the WCB is facing an apocalyptic deadline. The failing regional sector needs an astronomical amount of cash by the time the markets open on Monday, or they will declare bankruptcy. The Chairman must determine if it is even physically and legally possible to push enough money through the global financial network in time. 

They command their elite team of quantitative analysts to model the entire global banking system as a directed flow network. The WCB is the absolute source of the capital, and the failing regional sector is the ultimate sink. The analysts must input the strict daily transfer capacity limits of every single intermediary institution and every legal financial channel connecting them. 

The primary objective is to mathematically calculate the absolute maximum flow of liquidity. The WCB needs to know the highest possible total amount of money they can continuously pump from their reserves, through the tangled web of intermediaries, and into the failing sector every single day without violating the capacity limit of any single bank or transfer channel. 

If this calculated maximum throughput is lower than the amount needed to save the regional sector, the intervention will fail regardless of how much money the WCB actually holds in reserve. The money will simply pile up at the bottlenecks, causing regulatory gridlock while the target sector starves. By determining this absolute maximum financial flow, the Chairman can orchestrate a perfectly optimized, globally coordinated capital injection, saturating every available financial pathway simultaneously to pull the global economy back from the brink of total annihilation. The calculations must be ruthlessly precise. The 'conservation of capital' rule applies strictly at every intermediary node: an investment firm cannot magically create money, nor can it absorb it; the exact amount of emergency liquidity flowing into a clearing house must flow out to the next nodes in the chain. The analysts race against the clock, mapping the hidden, theoretical limits of the global financial architecture to find the exact saturation point of the world's economy.
