The orbital colony "Horizon's Edge" has suffered a catastrophic biological containment breach in its agricultural sector. An aggressive, airborne engineered pathogen is rapidly spreading through the station's complex ventilation and transit corridors. The station AI immediately activated emergency protocols, but the human oversight committee panicked and began issuing conflicting orders. The chief medical officer demanded a "maximum flow of breathable air" analysis, desperately trying to calculate the exact cubic meters of clean oxygen that could be pumped from the central life-support core into the infected zones to dilute the pathogen. They treated the crisis as a fluid dynamics problem, trying to maximize the throughput of clean air through the vents. 

Simultaneously, the security chief ordered the security drones to map the "shortest path to the escape pods." He was fixated on measuring the physical distance in meters from the agricultural sector to the nearest airlocks, believing that if they could just identify the fastest evacuation route for the remaining uninfected personnel, they could save them. He spent precious minutes calculating the exact sprint times of a standard human crew member down the various zero-G corridors. 

However, the station's lead structural engineer, trapped in the command center, realized that both of these approaches were suicidal. While it might seem like maximizing the flow of clean air, or finding the absolute shortest path for personnel to escape is the most logical priority in a crisis, these actions ignore the fundamental nature of the pathogen. Pumping more air just accelerates the spread, and opening paths for evacuation risks venting the pathogen into the escape pods themselves. 

The true problem is a matter of isolation and time. The station is a sprawling web of corridors, access shafts, and ventilation ducts. The command center must be completely severed from the agricultural sector to ensure the survival of the station's core crew. The engineer has remote control over the station's heavy emergency bulkheads. However, welding these massive blast doors permanently shut takes a significant amount of power and time, and the pathogen is moving fast. 

Therefore, the lead engineer is NOT trying to maximize the flow of clean air. They are NOT trying to find the shortest path to the escape pods, and they are NOT trying to sequence the evacuation chronologically. 

Instead, they need an algorithm to analyze the entire, interconnected web of the station's architecture and the specific welding time required for every individual bulkhead door. The objective is to identify the absolute perfect choke point. The algorithm must calculate the specific combination of bulkheads that, if welded shut, will completely sever all physical and atmospheric connection between the agricultural sector and the command center. Crucially, this specific set of bulkheads must represent the absolute minimum total welding time necessary to achieve complete isolation. The goal is to find the weakest link in the station's internal connectivity—the exact structural cut that separates the two zones using the absolute minimum total effort before the pathogen breaches the core.
